Cyclical dividends: when a high yield is a warning

Shipping, oil, mining and fertiliser companies often pay their biggest dividends right at the top of their cycle. Why the yield misleads at the top

Many cyclical companies don’t pay a fixed dividend. Shipping companies, some oil producers and several miners pay out a share of what they earn each quarter or year, so the dividend rises and falls with the cycle. When freight rates or commodity prices are at a peak, earnings are too, and so are the dividends.

The yield quoted on most websites is backward-looking: it is last year’s dividends divided by today’s price. At the top of the cycle that number can look extraordinary, while the market, which looks ahead, has already started to price in lower earnings. At the bottom it works the other way: dividends are small or suspended and the yield looks unattractive just when the driver is historically cheap.

That is why the driver’s position in its own history is a useful cross-check. A very high yield while the driver is near the top of its 20-year range deserves more questions than the same yield with the driver in its normal range.

What the driver’s position can suggest for the dividend

LowDriver low in its history

Earnings are often depressed, so dividends are often low, cut or suspended. The yield looks unattractive.

Normal rangeDriver in its normal range

Dividends are more likely to reflect mid-cycle earnings. The direction of the driver matters most here.

HighDriver high in its history

Earnings and dividends are often strong. Check how much of the payout depends on today’s prices; high prices have often not lasted.

Before buying a high yielder

  • Read the dividend policy. A fixed dividend, a share of earnings or a share of free cash flow behave very differently when earnings fall.
  • Look at the payout ratio. A company paying out more than it earns can’t keep that up for long.
  • Check the balance sheet. Debt and upcoming investments decide whether the company can keep paying through a downturn.
  • Check the driver. Open the signal page to see where the price sits against its history and how fast it is moving.

Questions

Why does a high dividend yield on a cyclical stock need care?

A trailing dividend yield divides the dividends paid over the past year by today’s share price. For a cyclical company, those dividends were paid out of recent earnings, which may have been unusually high. If the price behind those earnings is now at a historical high, the dividend can fall sharply once it turns, and the yield you see today won’t be repeated.

Why are there no yield figures on this page?

Yields change with every share price move and every dividend announcement, and Signycle has no live source for them. Rather than show figures that may be out of date, the page shows where each company’s main driver sits in its own history, which is the part Signycle can keep current.

Is a stock with a low yield and a driver low in its history a good buy?

Not necessarily. A low dividend while the driver is low is what you would expect near the bottom of a cycle, but the company also needs a balance sheet strong enough to get through the downturn, and prices can stay low for years. Signycle does not give buy or sell recommendations.

Why are some drivers marked “not tracked”?

A few companies depend on prices Signycle doesn’t track, such as coal or LPG freight. They are still listed because the dividend logic applies to them too, but there is no live reading to show.
