How Hormuz risk spreads through markets

When traffic through the Strait is threatened, the first effect is usually on oil: Brent rises and tends to pull away from WTI, which is priced in the US. Tanker rates can follow, as ships wait, reroute or pay more for war-risk insurance. LNG from Qatar uses the same route, so gas shipping is exposed too, and Gulf fertiliser exports add a link to farm costs.

When the risk fades, these moves can unwind at different speeds. Oil prices can fall quickly, while freight rates often take longer to normalise because ships and insurance need time to return to their usual patterns.

Questions

Why does the Strait of Hormuz matter for markets?

It is the narrow sea passage between the Persian Gulf and the Gulf of Oman, and the main export route for oil and gas from several Gulf producers. According to the US Energy Information Administration, roughly a fifth of the world’s oil consumption has passed through it in recent years, so even a threat to traffic can move prices.

Which stocks are most exposed?

Oil producers outside the Gulf tend to benefit from higher Brent, tanker owners from higher VLCC rates and LNG shipping companies from higher LNG rates, while airlines, refiners and chemicals producers face higher costs. Each signal page lists the stocks most tied to it.

Does this page say what is happening in the region now?

No. It shows where the most exposed signals stand against their own history, updated automatically. It does not report or interpret news. Hand-updated signals show the date of their last value.
